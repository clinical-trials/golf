One product, not two. On the range it diagnoses your swing from video, prescribes work in your pro's method, and retests to see whether anything moved. On the course it knows the hole in front of you and knows how you actually miss. Same judgment in both places, because that's what a real professional is.

The other half

On the range, the same pro.

The course advice above is only as good as the profile behind it, and that profile comes from video. Eight shots, three swings each, unedited — driver and mid-iron from both angles, a pitch, a chip, and two putting views. Level is assessed per domain, because full swing, short game, and putting genuinely diverge, and the gap between them is usually the whole story.

The dispersion driving every recommendation on this page is the same object the diagnosis engine writes to. Fix the fault on the range and the pattern on the course tightens. That loop is the product.

Building on CoachNow

What a communication hub can't do.

CoachNow is a good tool for what it is: per-athlete spaces, video markup with angle tools, a reusable drill library, batch messaging. Its unit of value is a message sent. It proves the coach communicated; it never proves the student improved. Everything below follows from changing that unit.

  • They measure messages

    Measure strokes instead

    Round data joins the coaching relationship, so the agenda is set by where you actually lose shots — not by what you felt like booking. Renewal stops being a sales conversation and becomes a chart.

  • Reports arrive as dead PDFs

    Keep the numbers longitudinal

    Launch monitor and motion data land as structured measurements, not attachments. You can ask whether a number has moved in six months and get an answer.

  • Capture is the student's problem

    Guide the camera

    Framing guides, automatic swing detection, auto-trim, club tagging. The coach stops spending goodwill asking for reshoots, and consistent framing is what makes a progress curve mean anything.

  • Async review is unpaid labour

    Draft it, don't author it

    Pose estimation and fault detection produce a draft in the pro's own voice; he approves or corrects in seconds. Every correction is stored as a labelled example, so the system sounds more like him over time.

  • The swing is all they see

    Own the course too

    Range mechanics and on-course scoring are the same relationship. Nobody joins them, which is why golfers hit it well on the range and badly where it counts.

  • Ninety numbers after four hours

    Photograph the card

    Round entry is a photo of the scorecard. The reader proposes, you confirm, and low-confidence cells are flagged rather than silently accepted. Nothing is written until you say so.

What this is

Real where it says real.

Genuinely real

  • Every course, hole, par, stroke index and hazard on this page is real OpenStreetMap data, fetched by scripts/fetch-courses.mjs in this repository.
  • Eight courses across four continents, 144 holes, all graded detailed.
  • The recommendation is computed, not written: 600 Monte Carlo shots per club against the actual hole polygons, scored on expected strokes.
  • Change the golfer and the answer changes, because the dispersion changes.

Not real

  • The three golfers. Their dispersions are invented to demonstrate the engine.
  • The expected-strokes model is a rough approximation, not a calibrated baseline. Real figures come from accumulated rounds.
  • No swing has been assessed. The range half is described, not built.
  • Kasumigaseki Country Club is missing because Overpass rate-limited the ninth request. It was left out rather than faked.
  • Some courses have no fairway polygons in OpenStreetMap, so every shot there reads as rough and the fairway percentage collapses to near zero. Royal County Down is the clearest case. That is a data gap, not a golfing verdict, and it is exactly what the coverage grades in the spec exist to make visible.
